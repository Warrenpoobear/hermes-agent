## Claim
NBER Working Paper 35574 ("AI Financial Advice: Supply, Demand, and Life Cycle Implications") finds most people would be financially better off following LLM-generated financial advice (tested against GPT-5.2 and Gemini 3 Flash) than their status-quo behavior, though advice quality varies meaningfully with how questions are framed/asked. A contemporaneous, methodologically distinct University of Bayreuth experiment (3,700 participants, fictional fund savings-plan choices) found the opposite risk holds too: AI advice can materially help when correct but can "strongly steer people in the wrong direction" when its recommendation is biased.

## Why it matters
This is a credible disagreement between two informed, independent studies on the same underlying question (how good is AI financial advice, and for whom) rather than a single vendor claim — exactly the kind of paired evidence the Skeptics/Evaluators weighting exists to surface. It is directly adjacent to (but methodologically distinct from) the already-tracked Vals AI Finance Agent v2 benchmark (institutional analyst tasks, ~52-60.6% accuracy ceiling): this is retail/household financial advice quality, not institutional analyst-task benchmarking, so it does not re-elevate that item, but it is a useful second data point on how much epistemic trust to place in AI-generated financial reasoning generally before any IC-drafting pilot.
